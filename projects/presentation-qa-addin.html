Presentation QA Add-in

An LLM-powered PowerPoint add-in I helped build and ship in a two-week sprint, turning a flagged client pain point into a €270K follow-on engagement.

The Challenge

The client, an audit firm, had already flagged this during working sessions on a six-week AI strategy engagement: their consultants were losing hours manually checking that every number, chart and claim in a final FDD deliverable matched the underlying source data before it went out the door. An AI tool wouldn't stop people checking their own work, but it could cut down how much of that checking had to happen by hand, and the repetitive effort behind it was real on every single deliverable, with one error in front of a client being costly.

The specifics of the client engagement are confidential, but the underlying problem is a common one in fast-moving advisory work: quality control that scales linearly with headcount instead of getting easier over time.

The Approach

The use case came out of that same strategy engagement. Rather than write it up as a recommendation for someone else to build, I proposed taking a first stab at it myself, working with a small team under supervision from senior consultants. I helped scope the checks and design the QA logic, and we shipped an end-to-end LLM-powered add-in (GPT-5 mini) in a two-week sprint, from first line of code to something the team was actually using day to day.

The add-in ran four checks across a deck: numeric and percentage calculations, chart and figure accuracy (checking that the data points in a graph matched what was reported around it), and content coherence, the hardest of the four, checking that titles, subtitles, diagram titles, footnotes, sources and tables lined up with the commentary written next to them and that the deck read as one consistent story. Because these were FDD deliverables, we had to prompt the LLM to reason like an accounting specialist, understanding balance sheet items and quality-of-earnings concepts well enough to catch a mismatch. For the numeric checks specifically, the LLM reasoned its way to slightly-off answers instead of just calculating, so we added a calculator function it called for anything arithmetic and kept the LLM focused on judgment calls like coherence.
